Comparing prices online usually means landing on stores you've never heard of. Most of them are legitimate, but fake shops and phishing "deals" are common enough that a few minutes of checking before you pay is worth it.

This guide covers how to vet an unfamiliar seller, which payment choices give you more options if things go wrong, and what to do if they do.

## Be skeptical of prices that are far below everyone else

When one site lists an item at a fraction of what every major retailer charges, that gap is the first warning sign. Real sales happen, but they rarely undercut the entire market by half or more on popular, current products.

Prices that deserve a closer look:

- Brand-new, in-demand electronics, sneakers, or designer goods at steep discounts.
- "Clearance" or "closing down" sales on a site with no history.
- Prices that only appear through an ad on social media and not anywhere else.
- Gift cards sold below face value by unofficial sellers.

To check, search the exact product name or model number and see what established retailers charge. If you want a method for judging whether a sale price is real, see [how to tell whether a discount is actually good](/guides/how-to-tell-if-a-discount-is-good/).

## Check the web address carefully

Fake stores often imitate real ones with lookalike domains. Before entering any payment information, read the full address in your browser's address bar, not just the logo on the page.

Common tricks include:

- **Extra words:** a known brand name plus "outlet," "sale," "official," or "store."
- **Swapped or added characters:** a letter replaced with a similar-looking number, a doubled letter, or a hyphen added.
- **Unusual endings:** a familiar name followed by an unexpected domain ending instead of the one the brand normally uses.
- **Subdomain tricks:** the real brand name appears at the start of the address, but the actual domain is something else further along.

The padlock icon or "https" only means the connection is encrypted. It doesn't mean the site is trustworthy, since scam sites can have certificates too.

A safer habit: go to a retailer by typing its address yourself or using a bookmark, rather than following a link from an ad, text, or email.

## Look for real contact information and policies

Legitimate stores generally make it easy to find out who they are and how to reach them. Before buying from an unfamiliar site, look for:

1. **A physical business address.** Search it. An address that turns out to be a residence, a mailbox service, or an unrelated business is a concern.
2. **A working phone number or support email** using the store's own domain, not only a contact form.
3. **A clear return and refund policy.** Vague, missing, or copy-pasted policies are a red flag. Policies that require returns shipped overseas at your expense deserve extra thought. Our guide on [how to check a return policy](/guides/how-to-check-a-return-policy/) explains what to look for.
4. **Shipping details** that say where items ship from and how long delivery takes.
5. **Consistent, specific writing.** Odd grammar, mismatched product photos, and pages full of placeholder text suggest a site that was put together quickly.

It also helps to search the store's name along with words like "reviews," "scam," or "complaints." Look at a range of sources, and be wary of sites where every review is glowing and posted around the same dates.

## Choose a payment method with protections

How you pay affects what you can do if an order never arrives or isn't what was described.

| Payment method | General level of recourse |
|---|---|
| Credit card | Usually the strongest. The Fair Credit Billing Act gives you the right to dispute certain billing errors, including charges for goods you didn't receive or that weren't delivered as agreed. |
| Debit card | Some protections for unauthorized transactions, but disputes over merchandise depend largely on your bank's policies, and the money has already left your account. |
| Established payment services | Many offer their own buyer protection programs with specific rules and deadlines. Check the terms. |
| Wire transfer, gift cards, cryptocurrency, payment apps meant for friends and family | Very limited recourse. Once sent, money is often hard or impossible to recover. |

A store that pushes you toward gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or a person-to-person payment app, especially with a discount for doing so, is showing a major warning sign. Legitimate retailers almost always accept standard card payments.

If you shop at many unfamiliar sites, some card issuers offer virtual card numbers that can be limited or turned off later, which can reduce the fallout if a site turns out to be fraudulent.

## Know who you're buying from on marketplaces

Large online marketplaces host many third-party sellers alongside the platform's own inventory. The listing page may look the same either way, but the seller, return policy, and shipping can differ a lot.

Before buying from a marketplace:

- **Find the "sold by" and "ships from" details.** These may be two different companies.
- **Look at the seller's profile**, including how long they've been active and their recent ratings.
- **Read the return terms** for that seller, since they may differ from the marketplace's standard policy.
- **Be cautious with brand-new sellers** offering popular items at prices well below other listings.
- **Keep communication and payment on the platform.** A seller who asks you to pay or message them elsewhere is trying to step outside the marketplace's protections.

Marketplace guarantees can help if something goes wrong, but they usually apply only to purchases made and paid for through the platform itself.

## Watch for phishing "deal" texts and emails

Scammers often pose as well-known retailers, delivery companies, or deal sites to get you to click a link. Common versions include:

- "You've won a gift card" or "Claim your reward" messages.
- Notices about a package you weren't expecting, asking you to confirm details or pay a small fee.
- Exclusive "VIP" or "employee" discount codes that require you to log in through a link.
- Order confirmations or cancellation notices for purchases you didn't make, urging you to call a number or click to dispute.

Some basic habits help:

- Don't click links in unexpected messages. Open the retailer's site or app directly and check your account there.
- Check the sender's full email address, not just the display name.
- Never give out passwords, one-time verification codes, or full card numbers in response to a message.
- Be wary of urgency, such as "within 24 hours" or "your account will be closed."

The FTC's consumer site at [consumer.ftc.gov](https://consumer.ftc.gov/) has general guidance on recognizing phishing and online shopping scams.

## What to do if something goes wrong

If an order never arrives, the item is clearly counterfeit, or you realize you've paid a fake store, act quickly. Deadlines for disputes are limited.

1. **Collect your records.** Save the order confirmation, product page screenshots, emails, tracking information, and any messages with the seller.
2. **Contact the seller first** if it appears to be a real business, and keep a written record of what you asked for and when.
3. **Dispute the charge with your card issuer or payment service.** Explain what happened and provide your records. Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, credit card billing disputes generally need to be sent in writing within 60 days after the statement containing the charge was sent, so don't wait. Payment services and banks set their own deadlines.
4. **If you shared card details with a fake site,** ask your card issuer about canceling the card and issuing a new number.
5. **If you entered a password,** change it on that account and anywhere else you reused it, and turn on two-step verification where available.
6. **Report it.** You can report fraud and scams to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). Reports help the agency spot patterns and bring cases, though the FTC generally doesn't resolve individual complaints. You can also report a fake listing or seller to the marketplace or platform where you found it.

This is general information, not legal advice. For a large loss or a complicated situation, your card issuer, your state attorney general's consumer protection office, or a qualified professional can give guidance specific to you.

## Quick safety checklist

Before you pay an unfamiliar store:

- [ ] Is the price in a believable range compared with established retailers?
- [ ] Did I type the address or use a bookmark, and does the domain look exactly right?
- [ ] Does the site list a real address, working contact details, and a clear return policy?
- [ ] Have I searched the store name plus "reviews" or "scam"?
- [ ] On a marketplace, do I know who the seller is and what their return terms are?
- [ ] Am I paying with a credit card or another method with buyer protections?
- [ ] Is the store asking for gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, or off-platform payment? If so, stop.
- [ ] Did this deal come from an unexpected text or email? If so, go to the store directly instead.
