A discount is only as good as the price it's measured against. "40% off" sounds generous, but if the starting price was never one anyone paid, the savings may be smaller than they look, or not there at all.

The checks below separate a real deal from a dressed-up regular price. None take long.

## Start with the reference price

Most sale tags show two numbers: the price you'll pay and a higher "reference" price labeled something like "was," "list," "MSRP," "regular," or "compare at." The discount percentage is calculated from that higher number, so it's worth asking where it came from.

### What the common labels usually mean

- **Was / Regular price:** Usually the store's own earlier price. The question is how long the item actually sold at that price, and how recently.
- **List price / MSRP:** The manufacturer's suggested retail price. Some product categories rarely sell at MSRP, so a discount from it can be routine rather than special.
- **Compare at / Compare to:** Often a price from somewhere else, or a price for a "comparable" product. This label is the vaguest of the group, and the comparison may not be to the exact same item.

### Signs a reference price may be inflated

The FTC has long-standing guidance on deceptive pricing that covers comparisons to former prices, but enforcement is uneven and sellers interpret it differently. As a shopper, watch for:

- An item that seems to be "on sale" every time you visit.
- A reference price far above what other major retailers charge for the identical model.
- Store-brand or exclusive products with no outside price to compare against, paired with very large percentage discounts.
- Countdown timers or "ends tonight" banners on a sale that keeps renewing.
- "Compare at" prices with no explanation of what was compared.

None of these prove a price is misleading. They are a cue to check what the item actually sells for before treating the percentage as meaningful.

## Check what the item really sells for

The most useful question isn't "how much is it marked down?" but "how does this price compare to what it usually costs?"

1. **Search the exact model number.** Product names vary between stores, but model numbers and SKUs usually don't. Searching the full model number is the quickest way to find the same item elsewhere.
2. **Look at two or three other retailers.** You're not hunting for the absolute lowest price, just a sense of the normal range.
3. **Use a price history tool if one covers the store.** Several browser extensions and websites track prices over time for large retailers and show charts of past prices. They don't cover every store or product, and their data can have gaps, but a chart that shows the "sale" price has been the normal price for months tells you a lot.
4. **Watch for model-year changes.** In electronics and appliances especially, a deep discount sometimes means a newer version just came out. That can still be a good buy, but compare it to the new model's price and features, not just the old list price.

## Percent off vs. dollars off

Percentages and dollar amounts can make the same savings feel different. A useful rule of thumb: on inexpensive items, percentages make savings look bigger; on expensive items, dollar amounts often do.

Here is a simple hypothetical comparison:

| Example offer | Item price | Savings | You pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25% off | $40 | $10 | $30 |
| $15 off | $40 | $15 | $25 |
| 10% off | $600 | $60 | $540 |
| $50 off | $600 | $50 | $550 |

When a store offers a choice between a percentage and a flat amount (common with coupon codes), do the math at your actual cart total. The better option changes depending on how much you're spending.

## Compare unit prices for consumables

For groceries, household supplies, personal care items, pet food, and anything else you use up, the sticker price can mislead. Package sizes change, and a "sale" on a smaller package can cost more per ounce than the regular price on a larger one.

Many stores display a unit price (per ounce, per count, per load) on the shelf tag or product page. If they don't, divide the price by the quantity yourself.

**Example:** A 24-ounce bottle on sale for $6.00 costs 25 cents per ounce. A 40-ounce bottle at a regular $9.00 costs 22.5 cents per ounce. The non-sale bottle is the better value, as long as you'll use it before it expires.

Be careful that you're comparing the same unit. Some listings use "per count" for one size and "per ounce" for another, and multipacks sometimes contain smaller individual items than the single version.

## Add up the total cost

A low item price can be offset by everything that gets added at checkout. Before deciding a deal is good, look at:

- **Shipping and handling**, including whether free shipping requires a minimum order or a membership.
- **Sales tax**, which varies by location and is usually calculated on the discounted price.
- **Fees** such as service, installation, or "processing" charges.
- **Required accessories** that aren't included, like cables, filters, or batteries.
- **Return costs** if the item doesn't work out, such as return shipping or restocking fees.

Our guide to [comparing final prices with shipping and tax](/guides/compare-final-prices-shipping-tax/) covers this step in more detail. It's also worth a quick look at the store's return terms before you buy; see [how to check a return policy](/guides/how-to-check-a-return-policy/).

## Read bundles and multi-buy offers carefully

"Buy one, get one 50% off," "3 for $20," and product bundles can be good deals, but only when you'd use everything in them.

- **BOGO 50% off** works out to 25% off each item if both are full price, and the discount often applies to the lower-priced item.
- **Multi-buy pricing** ("2 for $5") sometimes gives the same per-item price even if you buy just one. Check the fine print or the shelf tag.
- **Bundles** with accessories can be useful, but the bundled extras may be lower-quality versions you'd replace anyway. Price the main item alone and decide whether the extras are worth the difference.
- **Gift-with-purchase offers** add value only if the gift is something you want.

A useful test: would you buy every item in the bundle at the bundled price if they were sold separately? If not, the savings are partly imaginary.

## Learn the timing patterns

Many product categories follow seasonal patterns. These aren't guaranteed, and they vary by store and year, but they're a reasonable guide to whether waiting might pay off.

- **Late November** (around Black Friday and Cyber Monday) brings broad sales across electronics, appliances, toys, and much more.
- **After major holidays** stores often clear seasonal merchandise, such as decorations, gift sets, and seasonal clothing.
- **End of season** tends to bring markdowns on clothing, outdoor furniture, grills, and seasonal sports gear as stores make room for new stock.
- **Back-to-school season** (roughly July through early September) often includes deals on laptops, school supplies, and dorm items. Students may also qualify for separate [student discounts](/discounts/student/).
- **New model releases** often push down prices on the previous version of phones, TVs, cars, and appliances.
- **Holiday weekends** like Memorial Day, the Fourth of July, and Labor Day are common times for mattress, appliance, and furniture sales.

If you don't need something right away and it's not on sale, it's reasonable to wait for one of these windows. If you need it now, a modest discount today may be worth more to you than a bigger one in three months.

## Watch for pressure tactics

A real deal holds up when you take a few minutes to check it. Some signs that urgency is doing more work than the price:

- Countdown timers that reset when you reload the page.
- "Only 2 left" messages that never change.
- Discounts that only appear after you hand over an email or phone number, with a short deadline attached.
- Sales that require you to sign up for a store credit card or paid membership to get the advertised price.

They're a reason to slow down. If an offer looks unusually good from an unfamiliar store, see [how to compare online deals safely](/guides/how-to-compare-online-deals-safely/) before you buy.

## Quick checklist

Before you click "buy," run through these:

- [ ] Do I know where the "was" or "compare at" price came from?
- [ ] Have I searched the exact model number at two or three other stores?
- [ ] If a price history tool covers this store, is today's price actually low?
- [ ] For consumables, have I compared the unit price?
- [ ] What's the total with shipping, tax, and any fees?
- [ ] For bundles or BOGO deals, will I use everything?
- [ ] Is a better sale season coming soon, and can I wait for it?
- [ ] Can I return it, and what would that cost?
- [ ] Would I buy this at this price if it weren't labeled as a sale?
