A discount only helps if the item works out, and the return policy decides what happens when it doesn't. Reading it takes a few minutes, and the best time to do that is before you place the order, not after the box shows up.

## Where to find the return policy

Most stores put their return terms in more than one place, and the short version is not always the whole story. Check these spots:

- **The site footer.** Look for links labeled "Returns," "Return Policy," "Help," or "Customer Service."
- **The product page.** Many stores show a line such as "Free 30-day returns," "Final sale," or "Not returnable" near the price or under shipping details. A product-specific note usually overrides the general policy.
- **The help center.** Searching "returns" often turns up category exceptions that the main policy page only mentions in passing.
- **Checkout and your order confirmation.** Some stores repeat the return window or list items in your order that are excluded.

Read the full policy page, not just a banner. The banner says "free returns"; the fine print says which items, which methods, and by when.

## When the return window starts

A "30-day return policy" can mean different things depending on what starts the clock.

- **From the purchase date.** The window begins when you place the order, even if the item takes a week or more to arrive.
- **From the delivery date.** The window begins when the carrier marks the package delivered. This is more forgiving for slow or backordered shipments.

Here is a hypothetical example. You order on November 1 and the item arrives November 10. With a 30-day window from purchase, your deadline is December 1. With a 30-day window from delivery, it is December 10.

Also check two smaller details:

- **Calendar days or business days.** Most policies use calendar days, but not all.
- **"Started by" or "received by."** Some stores only require you to start the return or drop off the package before the deadline. Others require the item to arrive at their warehouse by then, which means you need to ship it earlier.

## Holiday extended return windows

Many retailers extend their return windows for purchases made during the late-fall and holiday shopping season, often allowing returns until a set date in January.

Before relying on it, confirm:

- The exact purchase dates that qualify. An item bought one day before the start date may fall under the normal window.
- Whether certain categories are excluded or follow a shorter holiday schedule. Electronics are a common exception.
- Whether the extension applies to marketplace sellers or only to items sold by the store itself.

## Shorter windows for electronics and appliances

At many stores, the standard window does not apply to every category. Items that commonly have shorter windows or extra conditions include:

- Phones, tablets, laptops, cameras, and other electronics, especially once activated or opened
- Large appliances, which may need to be uninstalled and inspected, or may not be returnable once installed
- Mattresses and furniture, which may have their own trial or pickup rules
- Personal care items, swimwear, and undergarments, for hygiene reasons
- Custom, personalized, or made-to-order items
- Gift cards, digital downloads, and software once a code has been revealed or used

For an expensive gadget, find the category rule before assuming the general window applies.

## Final-sale and opened-item exclusions

Clearance and "final sale" items are often not returnable at all, or only for store credit. That label may appear on the product page and nowhere else, so look for it before you add the item to your cart.

Opened items are another common sticking point. Some stores accept opened items in like-new condition. Others accept only unopened items, charge a fee for opened ones, or reduce the refund if accessories, manuals, or packaging are missing. Open-box and refurbished items you buy may also carry their own return terms.

## Restocking fees

A restocking fee is an amount the store keeps from your refund, usually a percentage of the item price. Stores that charge one tend to apply it to opened electronics, large or heavy items, and special orders.

As an example with hypothetical numbers: on a $400 item with a 15% restocking fee, you would get $340 back, not $400. Some stores waive the fee if the item is defective or damaged on arrival, so check whether that exception exists and what proof they want.

## Return shipping costs

"Free returns" does not always mean free in every situation. Common patterns include:

- **Prepaid label at no charge.** The store covers return shipping.
- **Label cost deducted from your refund.** The store provides a label but subtracts a fee.
- **You arrange and pay for shipping.** You pick the carrier and keep the tracking number.
- **Free only in person.** Returns are free at a store location or partner drop-off point, but not by mail.

Also note that original shipping charges are often non-refundable, and large items may involve a pickup fee. Factor these costs in when you compare prices; our guide on [comparing final prices including shipping and tax](/guides/compare-final-prices-shipping-tax/) shows how to add them up.

## How you get your money back

The refund method matters as much as the window. Look for which of these the store offers:

- **Original payment method.** The money goes back to your card or account.
- **Store credit or a gift card.** You can only spend it at that store, and it may expire.
- **Exchange only.** You can swap for another size or color but not get money back.

Returns with a gift receipt, or without any receipt, often get store credit rather than a refund. Also check timing. Many stores issue a refund only after the item is received and inspected, and then your bank or card issuer may take additional days to post it. If you paid with a buy-now-pay-later plan, check that provider's rules on how refunds are applied and whether scheduled payments continue until the refund posts.

## Marketplace sellers have separate policies

Many large retail sites also sell items from independent third-party sellers. On the product page, look for "sold by" and "shipped by" details. If the seller is not the store itself, the seller's return policy may apply, and it can be stricter: shorter windows, buyer-paid shipping, or no returns.

Some platforms offer their own guarantee on top of a seller's policy, with conditions. For a summary of how a specific retailer handles this, see its page in our [store directory](/stores/). ## Membership tiers can change the window

Some retailers give members of their loyalty or paid membership programs longer return windows, free return shipping, or receipt-free returns. A few have tiers where higher-spending members get more generous terms.

Check whether you need to be signed in at purchase for the perk to apply, and whether it covers every category.

## Document your purchase

Policies change, and customer service agents work from what they can verify. Save these for every order you might return:

- The order confirmation email and any invoice or receipt
- The packing slip that came in the box
- Serial numbers for electronics and appliances
- Photos of the item and box on arrival, especially if anything looks damaged
- The original packaging and accessories, at least until the window closes

Set a calendar reminder a few days before the return deadline so you have time to decide and ship.

### What to screenshot before you buy

A screenshot shows what the terms looked like when you ordered. It does not guarantee a store will honor older terms, but it gives you something concrete to point to. Capture:

- The return policy page, with the date visible if possible
- The product page, showing the price, the seller name, and any "final sale" or return note
- The final checkout page with your total
- Any chat or email in which a representative promised something specific

## If a return goes wrong

Start with customer service and be specific: your order number, the date, and what the policy said. If that fails, ask for a supervisor or use the store's written complaint channel. If you paid by credit card, your card issuer may have a dispute process for charges you believe are in error; contact them promptly, since there are time limits. The FTC's consumer site at [consumer.ftc.gov](https://consumer.ftc.gov/) has general guidance on resolving problems with purchases.

## Return policy checklist

Before you check out, confirm:

1. How long the window is, and whether it starts at purchase or delivery
2. Whether the item's category has a shorter window or extra conditions
3. Whether the item is final sale or excluded once opened
4. Whether a restocking fee applies, and when it is waived
5. Who pays return shipping, and whether free returns require an in-store drop-off
6. Whether you get a refund to your original payment or store credit
7. Who the seller is, and whose policy applies
8. Whether a holiday extension or membership perk changes the deadline

After you buy, save your confirmation, take screenshots, keep the packaging, and set a reminder. A return policy is part of the price, which is why it belongs in any decision about [whether a discount is actually good](/guides/how-to-tell-if-a-discount-is-good/).
